# Legitimacy remains to come: Jacques Derrida and the Abyss of the State 

Nils Hofmann, University of Vienna


Can the state be justified if its foundation is based on violence? This chapter
develops a deconstructive account of the state in Jacques Derrida’s work through
the notion of legitimacy to come, caused by the abyss at the heart of its
violent founding. I argue that the state must be continually questioned in the
name of the other which it violently excludes at the moment of its founding. In
this way, Derrida’s thought opens a path towards rethinking democracy beyond
classical conceptions of sovereignty.

First, I examine Derrida’s appropriation of Heidegger’s concept of the abyss in
order to demonstrate that the constitutive power underlying the founding of the
state cannot itself be grounded in any ultimate signifier, such as popular
sovereignty or (natural) law,

Second, I trace how Derrida’s political writings develop the argument that
states necessarily presuppose the violent distinction from the other. Such
distinction is necessary for states to constitute themselves reflexively as
states. The state therefore remains answerable to its other. This reflexive
relation to the Other within the state’s own order becomes the site of its
deconstruction.

Third, I elaborate how this site of the state’s deconstruction paradoxically
entails both the possibility of sovereignty and the possibility of opening
towards the other in order to let justice come. Without an ultimate signifier,
whether a state’s decision is just can never be fully determined in the present.
Rather, it can be disclosed only in the mode of legitimacy to come, understood
through the future perfect. The state must justify its actions in light of what
will have been just. Finally, I outline two political approaches that follow
from Derrida’s questioning of the state.
